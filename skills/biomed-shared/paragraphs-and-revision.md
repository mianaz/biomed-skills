# Paragraphs and revision

Give each paragraph a clear message. Its evidence, comparison and interpretation
can work together to develop that message. Make the relationship between
sentences explicit through scientific reasoning. Use substantive subjects and
concrete verbs. Preserve numerical and technical meaning during compression.

Reuse established terminology across sections. Revise the passages implicated
by the user's request and any adjacent statements needed for consistency. Give
a structural change a scientific reason. After an addition, check whether an
existing passage already performs the same function and combine them where
appropriate. Preserve the ambition of the original idea while repairing its
logic, specificity or inferential scope.

For Chinese notes intended for an English manuscript, reconstruct the argument
in natural biomedical English. Preserve the author's scientific intent, figures,
quantities and terminology. Explain consequential changes in the language the
user requested.

## Concrete sentences and forward movement

Choose the sentence's subject from the paragraph's focal object and the preceding
sentence. Name the relevant cell population, treatment, measurement or result.
Replace abstract noun chains with a clear relationship between concrete entities.
Introduce the comparator and condition before asking the reader to
interpret a change; name the metric that increased or decreased. Preserve the
original values, units, reference group and direction. Query missing comparisons
instead of supplying a plausible one.

Split a sentence when it carries several reasoning steps: observation, local
inference and wider implication can occupy connected sentences. Keep a needed
qualification beside its claim. Do not impose a word limit or break a comparison
into fragments that make its meaning harder to follow.

## Focus, voice and rhythm

Before choosing active or passive voice, identify what the reader is following
and what the next sentence adds. Use the subject and information order to carry
that focus forward. Active voice can connect a mechanistic cause directly to its
effect. Passive voice can keep a transported antibody or an affected population
in subject position. An active sentence can also foreground the population while
placing the analytical method in a short introductory phrase.

These fictional language examples illustrate choices of focus:

| Paragraph focus | Sentence and reason |
|---|---|
| Mechanistic action | “Crosslinks obstruct DNA replication.” The cause leads directly to its effect. |
| Antibody trafficking | “The antibody was internalized and delivered to lysosomes.” The antibody remains the topic through successive destinations. |
| Cells reached by treatment | “Neighboring antigen-negative cells were also killed by conjugate A.” The affected population leads; the causal agent remains explicit. |
| Targets retained by a cell population | “In the profiling data, population M retained targets T1 and T2.” The population carries the finding; the method supplies context. |

Retain the actor, recipient and causal direction when changing voice. Keep an
agent explicit when its identity matters. Voice follows the local focus, so a
paragraph can mix active and passive sentences naturally. Vary sentence length
and structure with the complexity and emphasis of the reasoning. A short sentence
can state a decisive finding; a longer one can develop a connected comparison or
explanation. Use equivalent wording where it preserves the referent and meaning;
keep technical distinctions and entity names clear. Keep effective sentences
when they already serve the passage. Variation earns its place through emphasis,
flow or rhythm, with no fixed alternation pattern or quota.

## Paragraph progression

Give each paragraph a distinct contribution. Its closing sentence should add a
specific inference, mechanism or unresolved question only when that advances the
argument. End at the last substantive sentence when the point is already complete.
Remove endings such as “opens opportunities,” “complementary strategies” or
“different responses” when they merely rename what the paragraph has explained.
Keep these terms when the prose specifies the opportunity, strategy or response
and develops its biological consequence. Do not replace one stock ending with
another or force a prediction into every paragraph.

Check neighboring paragraphs for repeated motivation, findings and conclusions.
Introduce a motivation where it first becomes necessary; later paragraphs should
use it to advance the argument. Build transitions from the specific question or
inference that connects the paragraphs. Adding “Furthermore” or “Together” does
not repair a missing scientific connection.

Continuity can come from subject choice, information order, a shared term or a
question followed by its answer. Sentences and paragraphs can proceed directly
when their relationship is already clear. Add a separate transition sentence
when it supplies needed reasoning or orientation; remove one that only repeats
the preceding point or the next paragraph's opening.
