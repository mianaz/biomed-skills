# Argument bridges

Use when drafting or repairing an explanation, a transition, or a paragraph that
uses several studies to support one judgment. Read the relevant source passages
before adding a biological or pharmacological link.

## Connect the example to the judgment

Identify what the paragraph asks the reader to conclude. For each example, find
the observation and the property, comparison or premise that makes it informative
for that conclusion. Write the missing link where the inference occurs. Depending
on the study, that link can concern mechanism, experimental design, context
dependence or a measured pattern. A descriptive discovery can carry its own
scientific significance.

Keep each agent or intervention beside its relevant property and outcome. When
several drugs and toxicities appear, make their individual relationships clear
in the same clause or connected sentences. Parallel lists leave the reader to
guess the mapping. Check each relationship against its source; a reported
toxicity alone supplies no evidence for a particular causal mechanism.

For example, in a **fictional** source set, study A reports plasma release of
payload A and links that exposure to marrow toxicity; study B reports a stable
conjugate and target-dependent tissue injury. A useful synthesis preserves those
different explanatory paths. Listing both agents and then both toxicities loses
the pharmacological comparison.

## Supply the premise at a transition

Ask what scientific premise connects the preceding finding to the next question.
For a new experiment, state what it will distinguish or establish. For a review,
explain why the biological change or treatment history changes the next problem.
Use the smallest addition that makes that reasoning visible.

Separate a link reported in the current experiment, a relationship established
in cited work, and an author's proposed explanation. Use
[evidence and interpretation](evidence-and-interpretation.md) for the inferential
status. When the source leaves a link open, develop a clearly stated model if
the task calls for interpretation, or keep the sentence at its supported scope.
Preserve the discovery's interest while making the proposed link explicit.

## Earn the synthesis

Trace each dimension in a topic or closing sentence to a concrete example in the
paragraph. A conclusion about target biology needs relevant evidence such as
antigen distribution or uptake; examples about linker release alone support a
conclusion about release. Add relevant sourced evidence when needed, or focus the
conclusion on the relationship actually developed.

Introduce a concept through its concrete role: an appositive or short clause can
explain which property changes delivery, exposure or response. Prefer that local
explanation to a parenthetical dictionary definition. Retain established terms
and the original sentence backbone where they already work. Finish when the
reader can follow the example-to-judgment relationship and the transition without
supplying a consequential hidden premise. An already clear paragraph needs no
extra bridge or closing summary.
