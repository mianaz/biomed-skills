# State transitions and population dynamics

Use when time-dependent states or a mechanistic process are the target. Ordinary
group differences over repeated times first need a repeated-measures design model;
choose a process model when its states and transitions answer the actual question.

## Choose the process and observation model

| Data and question | Candidate | Establish first |
|---|---|---|
| Observed discrete state transitions | Markov chain | State definitions, time step, dependence horizon, stationarity and absorbing states |
| Noisy observations generated by latent states | Hidden Markov model | Initial, transition and emission models; sequence boundaries and state support |
| Population flows through disease states | SIR/SEIR or justified extension | Compartment meanings, rate units, mixing, immunity, population and observation process |

The Markov assumption makes the next state conditional on the present state,
given the modeled structure. Assess whether duration, history or a changing
environment requires additional structure. For a finite chain, irreducibility and
aperiodicity support the usual unique limiting-distribution result; a stationary
distribution alone does not guarantee convergence from every starting state.

## Fit and decode an HMM

Keep these outputs distinct:

- The forward calculation sums possible paths to obtain sequence likelihood.
- Forward-backward inference gives marginal state probabilities at each position.
- Viterbi decoding finds the most probable joint state path under the fitted model.

Estimate parameters with an established implementation. Baum–Welch/EM can converge
to a local optimum; assess multiple starts, convergence and held-out sequences.
Separate structurally impossible transitions from zeros caused by limited
observations. Use stable log or scaling computations and preserve boundaries
between independent sequences. Choose splitting for the intended use, including
future-time forecasting when relevant. Name latent states biologically only with
support from their emissions and independent evidence.

## Use compartment models for an explicit scenario or forecast

Define each state, allowed transitions, initial values, population accounting and
time unit. Tie rates to duration assumptions. Distinguish exposure/latent states
from infectious states and state how the model maps to observed cases, tests or
other measurements. Add compartments when both the question and identifying data
justify them. Check conservation/accounting and plausible limiting behavior.

A deterministic scenario gives the implications of chosen parameters. A fitted
forecast additionally needs an observation model, identified parameters, uncertainty
and an evaluation appropriate to the forecast horizon. Record structural, parameter
and intervention/implementation uncertainty. Assess sensitivity for the specific
decision and time horizon, including plausible alternative structures.

For a value-of-information question, specify actions, outcomes/loss, model weights
and horizon. Expected value of perfect information bounds the value of resolving
the modeled uncertainty; the value and cost of a particular study need a further
calculation. Use the columns' disease examples to understand mechanisms, and obtain
current disease-specific evidence for an applied health decision.

Deliver the state diagram, equations/transition definitions, assumptions,
parameter sources or fitting method, diagnostics and scenario/forecast uncertainty.
